From recent practice: what it looks like in real life

I went to build a knowledge base and built a document generator the same day

The group has several business lines. For one of them, I went to meet the best salesperson to build a knowledge base for the sales team. That was more of a long-term task: there were no immediate plans to hire into that team. Within the first 20 minutes it became clear that his main pain was the standard documents he has to produce during a sale. Four of them, with no templates at all. Fully manual work, with the same data retyped from one document to the next. It ate hours of a valuable specialist's time that he could have spent selling, which he does brilliantly.

The same day, a prototype document generator was built and tested. Then we held exactly one meeting with the team, collected feedback, polished it and put it into use. The result is a tool that saves hours every day, reduces errors and makes the work more enjoyable: the salesperson can focus on people instead of wrestling with paperwork.

A small thing with a big effect

I visit one of the departments that works alongside sales. It turns out the big problem is that information arrives either in private messages or by email. When people started setting tasks in Bitrix, they did it without any template, so the department constantly had to ask for missing details. We made one template task in Bitrix: a link to the instructions for salespeople on what to fill in and how, plus a checklist of required fields right in the task.

This simple template saved a lot of time: for salespeople, who now provide everything in one go and move on, and for the other department, which no longer chases details and can process the request right away. And that is with the team already having an outside Bitrix contractor, who works on request rather than proactively because they are not embedded in the processes the way we are.

    • Alexander Dubovenko Founder of Good Wood
      “

      Knowledge bases, regulations, checklists, instructions, standards are the so-called blue, "bureaucratic" layer of an organization. Without it, you can't grow any kind of systematic business. You can compare it to the skeleton of an organization. No skeleton, and you don't exist. Too much skeleton, and you've ossified, lost flexibility and the pace of growth.

      This book is about how an owner can stop telling every employee directly what to do and how, so that operations finally run on autopilot. For that, every new hire needs to be able to adapt quickly, and long-timers must not forget the standards.

      Many will think: "Why do we need a knowledge base if we have AI?" I'll tell you an unusual thing: AI needs regulations no less than people do. Here's a concrete example.
